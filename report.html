NYHP Project & Operations Compass

Final project report. MGT 409, AI Foundations for Managers.
Author: Artorius Reyes
GitHub repository: https://github.com/AMR6958-coder/nyhp-compass
The app online: https://nyhp-compass.onrender.com. It is a pilot copy that asks for a sign-in and holds fictitious data only. The app also runs on any computer with Python (three commands in the README).
In one sentence: Compass helps a hospital's facilities leaders and its CFO decide which building projects to do, and when, by scoring every project against the hospital's own priorities, working out the best plan that fits the yearly budgets, and using AI only to draft what a person then checks.

November 23, 2026. Every screenshot is of the app running on the demo hospital, New York General, which is fictitious. Every measured number says where it came from.

1. Motivation

Who has this problem. A hospital's facilities vice-president and its CFO. Each year they hold a list of dozens of building projects, such as roofs, chillers, air handlers, generators, windows and controls, that together cost many times what the hospital can spend. In New York City the list now carries a second clock: Local Law 97 sets a yearly limit on a large building's greenhouse gas emissions and charges $268 for every metric ton over it, with a tighter limit starting in 2030. A hospital runs around the clock, cannot shut a wing at will, and uses far more energy for its floor area than an office does, so it is exposed to the law and has less freedom than most owners in how it responds.

The public numbers. The median general hospital uses 234 kBtu of energy for each square foot in a year, about 4.4 times the median office (ENERGY STAR) [1]. Local Law 97 covers most buildings over 25,000 square feet, about 50,000 of them [3], and a not-for-profit hospital that applied in time has its limit set at 85% of its 2018 emissions through 2029 and 70% from 2030 [2]. The city's hospital association told the city in 2021 that even the hospitals most successful at cutting emissions would probably miss the targets and face significant penalties [4]. Meanwhile the buildings are ageing and the money is short: in a 2024 survey of more than 430 hospital facilities leaders, 80% named ageing facilities as their leading concern, and 79% said fewer than half of their deferred maintenance requests were funded [5]. And choosing is the hard part: in a 2024 survey of 102 health system finance leaders, the top barrier to effective capital planning was the inability to prioritize across several important initiatives, named by half of them [6]. A trade article this year put it more bluntly: the advocates who "squeak loudest get the grease" [7].

How they deal with it today. Today the list lives in spreadsheets and in the heads of a few people. An engineering study names the projects. Someone copies the costs and savings into a workbook by hand. A committee ranks them in a meeting, and the ranking reflects who argued best as much as what the hospital values. Whether the chosen projects fit the budget year by year, in an order that respects which must finish first, is worked out by trial and error. The tool this project started from was exactly that: an Excel workbook with an optimizer attached, made by others, which I built upon and credit in the README.

What it costs them. Three things. Time: reading a scope document and typing its projects in, then rating each one on dozens of factors, is days of a senior person's work for one planning cycle. Money: a plan that is merely feasible, when a better one existed under the same budget, leaves savings and avoided fines unclaimed for years. Trust: when the CFO asks why project A is ahead of project B, "the committee felt" is a weak answer. The reason anyone should care is that the money is large and public. In the demo hospital's list, 34 projects add up to $1.29 billion against budgets that can fund a fraction of them.

Why not just ask ChatGPT? Three pieces of evidence. In a 2026 test of six frontier models on 200 constrained optimization problems, checked against a real solver, the best model gave an answer that broke no rule 65% of the time, and none was both rule-abiding and near the best answer on more than 30.5% [17]. In a peer-reviewed 2025 study, seven assistants chose among 16 retrofit packages for 400 homes, and the best matched the top-ranked retrofit 54.5% of the time [18]. And the makers' own documentation says a model can state things that are wrong and will not always give the same answer twice [19]. To be fair in the other direction: an assistant that can run code could call a real solver, and the newest models are much better than those of 2023. The open questions are whether it does so for a given request, and whether it writes the model correctly, with no record kept either way.

What Compass does, in one tool.

  • The hospital's own scoring matrix. Resilience 40%, business 40%, disruption 20% (which counts against a project), each split into factors rated 0, 3, 6 or 10. The hospital sets the weights once; every project is scored the same way, and changing a weight re-scores everything.
  • A real optimizer for the plan. A mixed-integer program (not AI) picks the set of projects and their start years that scores highest within a capital budget and an operations budget, with must-do projects, prerequisites, package prices and phased work. It runs in about 4 seconds on the demo's 33 projects (measured twice tonight).
  • Local Law 97 by the rule book. Emissions limits and fines are computed in code from the published rules, year by year, with and without the plan.
  • AI kept in a narrow job. The model drafts and a person decides. A drafted rating must carry a quote from the hospital's own record, and code checks that the quote is really there.
  • Honest results. A low or negative score is shown as it is. The purpose is that a hospital can say "this is the best we can do" and show why.

Being honest about closeness. Every piece of this exists somewhere, in mature products with far more depth than a course project. Among the pages I read, none describes one tool that puts all four together: AI reading of project documents, the hospital's own scoring priorities, a mathematical optimizer for the multi-year plan, and Local Law 97 fines. The nearest are the asset investment planners, which share the method and serve other industries, and Brightly's planner, which groups projects with AI under a budget. What Compass adds is the combination, sized for one hospital's facilities team, and the rule that no AI-drafted rating stands without a sentence from the hospital's own record behind it.

3. The Agents

What They Are and What Each One Does

AI FeatureWhat Goes InWhat Comes OutMost Output Allowed
Scope reader (Read a Document)An engineer's scope narrative: PDF, Word or textDraft projects (name, cost, timing, savings and more), each figure with the sentence it came from. Anything doubtful is held for the person.32,000 tokens
Auto scoreOne project's own record and the hospital's scoring matrixA draft rating for each factor, each above "no impact" backed by a quote from the record. A factor the record says too little about is left blank.16,000 tokens
Drawing readerOne sheet of the hospital's floor plansDraft named areas with outlines, which a person accepts, so projects can be placed on the building.64,000 tokens

Tools each agent can use: none. This is deliberate. Each feature is one request to the model, streamed, with adaptive thinking on, that must answer in a fixed structure. The model cannot browse the web, cannot query the database, cannot read another hospital's data and cannot write anything. Code hands it the material and code checks what comes back. All three work under the same 17 written rules (rule set 2026-11), shown to the user in Settings. Rule 1 is the closed world: use only the hospital's records, the document attached to the task and the rule tables that ship with the app, and do not fill gaps from general knowledge or typical values.

How They Are Organized

Figure 1. The three AI features do not call each other. Each hands its draft to checking code and then to a person. What a person accepts goes into the database, and from there the optimizer and the reports (no AI) take over.

What is not AI. The plan optimizer, the Local Law 97 calculator, the energy model and load profile readers, and three of the scoring factors (how fast a project pays back, how long it takes to build, and demand response revenue), which code works out from the project's own figures.

Which model, and why. All three features call Claude Opus 5.5 (claude-opus-5-5). The job is careful reading of long, messy engineering text with exact quotes and a strict output structure, where a wrong figure could misdirect millions of dollars, so I chose the most capable model the features were tested with. At about 13 cents a run (measured below), the dearer model is not where the money is. The app itself was built with Claude in the Claude Code app; AI_Prompt.md in the repository logs every prompt I sent.

On the evening of November 23, 2026 I ran Auto score on seven projects of the demo hospital, one after another, between 9:08 and 9:23 PM. The app's own record of those runs is the table in section 4. The screens below show the steps.

For the first of the seven runs (Roof Replacement: Phase 4) the request carried 12,452 input tokens and the model returned 4,060 output tokens, for 13.1 cents. The score that results is on the same scale as every other project's, and the plan optimizer uses it without knowing a model was ever involved.

Cached input is zero, and I want to be straight about why. Compass does not use prompt caching today. The data shows the opening for it: input was between 12,332 and 12,452 tokens across seven different projects, a spread of 120 tokens, so nearly all of every request is the same text (the 17 rules and the wording of the scoring matrix). But caching reuses a request from its start, and today the changing project record is sent ahead of the long, unchanging list of factors. So the fix is two steps: move the fixed text to the front, then turn caching on. Output is the larger part of the bill in any case, 57% to 62% of each run.

What One Run Costs, and a User in a Month

  • One run: 12.5 cents on average (11.6 to 13.1 cents across the seven).
  • One user in a month, my estimate: rating a list of 35 projects once is 35 runs, or about $4.40. A user who re-drafts a third of them and reads a few documents lands near $6 to $8.
  • The limit: a pilot login is stopped once it has spent $20 in a calendar month. The check runs before a run, so the run that reaches the limit is allowed to finish and a month can end a few cents over. That is enforced in code and tested.

What I Did to Keep Cost Down

  • The plan costs nothing in tokens. The optimizer is ordinary mathematics on the user's own machine or server. The dearest question in the app, "what is the best plan?", never touches a model.
  • Code does what code can. Three scoring factors come from the project's figures by rule, so the model is never asked for them. One factor that has no wording in the hospital's template is never drafted at all.
  • One request for each project, covering all its remaining factors, instead of one request for each factor.
  • A cap on output for each feature, a size limit on uploads (20 MB), and a per-login limit of $20 a month.
  • A rough cost is shown before the run for Auto score ("roughly 10 to 20 cents") and for a drawing, and the user must say yes. All eight measured Auto score runs fell inside that range. Reading a document shows that it will be sent to the AI service, but no dollar figure: a gap.
  • A demo reader replays a saved reading of the sample document, so showing document reading to people costs nothing. Auto score runs at medium effort; the two readers at high.

Did Any Cost Cutting Make the App Worse?

I have no evidence that it did, and I have not run the comparison that would settle it. The one quality fault a live run exposed was not a saving. In the first drawing reading every outline came back about 23% too small, because the picture Compass sent was larger than the service looks at, so the service shrank it and the corner points came back on the smaller picture's scale. After the fix, the second reading put 67 of 69 outlines within 90% of the true rooms (the sample drawing's true geometry is known because it was drawn from code). The fix sent a smaller picture and the cost did not fall: about 18 cents before and 19 after. Two readings of one clean sheet are not a controlled test.

Where it did not: working three factors out by rule loses nothing, because a rule gives the same exact answer every time and is covered by tests. Where I do not know: I did not run the same projects at different effort settings or on a cheaper model and compare the ratings, so I cannot claim a cheaper model would do as well. And three checks were left undone because each costs model calls: the trap documents have not been run against the live model, a planned "second look" (an extra request for each document that reads a picture of each page, to catch what text extraction misses) is not built, and no document has been read live since the defenses were added. Each of those is a place where spending less may be costing quality, and I cannot yet say.

5. Safety

Attacks: A Document That Tries to Give Orders

The scope reader takes in documents written by someone else, so a document can carry planted instructions: white text, text pushed off the page, hidden Word text, invisible characters, or a plain sentence addressed to "the AI". Compass treats every upload as data. Before the model sees it, code sets aside text a person could not see (tiny, same colour as its background, off the page, drawn with no ink, hidden in Word) and takes out invisible characters. A document with a sentence addressed to an AI is held for a person. After the model answers, code looks up the quote behind every figure in the visible text: a figure that rests on hidden text or on a sentence aimed at an AI is refused and the draft is held, a figure whose quote cannot be found is marked "not found in document" for the reviewer, and any link in the model's own words is removed. The repository holds a set of 11 trap documents (samples/scope_traps): eight attacks, two clean decoys and one scanned page, with a key that says what each should produce.

Agent Malfunction: Invented Facts and Runaway Output

  • No quote, no rating. In Auto score, a rating above "no impact" whose quote is not in the project's record is dropped by code, not by the model's good manners. In the scope reader, code does the arithmetic itself ("18 months" becomes 1.5 years), and where a document gives two figures for one fact it uses the one least favourable to the hospital and holds the field for a person.
  • Blank is allowed. The model is told to leave a factor blank when the record does not say enough, and blanks are shown as blanks.
  • A person saves. A draft changes no score and no plan until a person checks it and presses Save. A draft with a held item cannot be saved at all until the reviewer confirms it. Imported gaps are written into a project's name, such as "(cost not stated)", so they cannot hide.
  • No loops. Each feature is a single request with a cap on output. There is no agent loop to run away, and a reply cut off at the cap is refused, not half-used. A scope document is limited to 600 pages and 800,000 characters.
  • Each login sees only its own data. Two separate checks keep one login out of another's workspaces, and a test walks every address in the app to prove it.

Overspending

Each login has an allowance of $20 a calendar month. At the limit the three AI features stop with a clear message until the next month, and everything else keeps working. The limit is checked before a run and the cost counted after it, so the run that reaches the limit is allowed to finish and a login can end a month slightly over. On a copy with logins, sign-in locks for 15 minutes after five wrong passwords, a run can only be started by a signed-in person pressing a button, and every change and every AI run is written to an activity record.

A Defense Working: The Tests

These are real results from tonight. The two test files below cover the document defenses and the spending limit: 37 tests, all passing. The whole suite, 290 tests, passes too.

$ python -m pytest -q server/tests/test_scope_defenses.py server/tests/test_ai_budget.py -v
...
============================= 37 passed in 2.13s ==============================

In plain words: one trap document hides a "correction" in white 1-point text that cuts a cost from $4,800,000 to $1,200,000. The test runs it through the same reader the app uses and asserts the line is held back as "tiny text (1.0 pt)". It then feeds in the answer a fooled model would give, $1,200,000, and asserts the checks refuse it, leave the cost blank and hold the draft. Another test spends a login up to its limit and asserts the next run is refused with status 403. These tests call no model: they prove the code around the model.

What I have not proven. The trap documents are tested against the defenses in code. They have not yet all been run against the live model, because each run costs money and I ran out of time. The "second look" is not built. I say so here so nobody reads more into the tests than they show.

7. Limits and Next Steps

What Does Not Work Yet

  • It has only just gone online. The pilot copy was first deployed on November 24, 2026, as one service with a disk, and nobody but me has used it yet. PostgreSQL, which a larger deployment would need, has never been run with it.
  • Fictitious data only. No real hospital has given permission for its documents or drawings, so the AI features have not met a real, old, scanned drawing.
  • Safety gaps: the trap set has not been run in full against the live model; the "second look" is not built; sign-in has no second proof of identity and no roles.
  • No prompt caching, although the measurements show most of each request repeats. No comparison of models or effort settings.
  • Construction tracking is typed in by a person. A connection to Procore is planned and not built.
  • Local Law 97: offsets, campus plants and credit for existing equipment are not handled, and my reading of the heat pump credit awaits confirmation.

With Another Month

  1. Run the four-login pilot on the hosted copy, and replace my estimates in section 6 with what real users did.
  2. Move the unchanging text to the front of each request, turn on prompt caching, and measure the saving on the same seven projects.
  3. Run all 11 trap documents against the live model and publish the results, pass or fail, then build the second look.
  4. Compare models and effort settings on the same projects, so the choice of model rests on evidence.
  5. With a hospital's permission, read real scope documents and real drawings.
  6. Connect construction tracking to Procore.
